# Evidence, Strengths, and Opportunity Scoring

## Strength Evidence Score

Score each candidate strength from 0–3 on five dimensions. Explain every score with evidence; do not create false precision.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Repetition | No example | One vague example | One clear or several partial examples | Repeated across settings or time |
| Outcome proof | None | Subjective impression | Observable result | Quantified or durable result |
| External recognition | None | Compliment | Request, adoption, or referral | Payment, repeat use, promotion, or strong reliance |
| Relative ease | Unknown | Mainly effort | Some method or speed advantage | Clear and repeatable comparative advantage |
| Transferability | Highly context-bound | One adjacent use | Several plausible uses | Demonstrated across contexts |

Interpretation:

- **12–15:** high-confidence strength
- **8–11:** strong lead; gather more evidence
- **4–7:** possible strength; do not build the main recommendation on it
- **0–3:** insufficient information

A score estimates evidence strength, not personality, worth, income, or certainty of success.

## Opportunity Score

Rate 1–5 and provide one-line reasoning for each factor:

1. **Personal fit:** evidence, preference, energy, and working style.
2. **Demand evidence:** actual payment and repeated requests outrank trends or intuition.
3. **Startup cost:** money, time, tools, credentials, and access.
4. **Speed to revenue:** realistic time to first paid proof.
5. **Sustainability:** repeatability, margin, learning, differentiation, and owner workload.
6. **Risk:** financial, employment, legal, privacy, reputation, health, and dependency risks. A higher score means safer.

Do not let the total score hide a fatal constraint. State disqualifiers separately. Rank no more than three main opportunities and keep other ideas in a short option pool.

## Evidence Ladder for Demand

From weakest to strongest:

1. Personal interest or broad trend.
2. Views, likes, compliments, or survey intent.
3. Specific problem conversations or repeated requests.
4. User gives time, data, access, contact details, or agrees to a pilot.
5. Pre-order, deposit, signed trial, or first payment.
6. Repeat purchase, referral, retention, or price acceptance.

Label levels 1–2 as hypotheses, not validated demand.

## Low-Cost Validation Menu

Choose the smallest experiment that can disprove the key assumption:

- Five focused conversations with plausible buyers.
- Three pieces of content addressing the same narrow problem.
- A manual sample or before/after demonstration.
- A one-page offer sent to a small relevant audience.
- A paid pilot with narrow scope and a clear boundary.
- A pre-order or refundable deposit before building a product.
- A simple referral or affiliate test using an existing audience.

Define for every experiment:

- hypothesis;
- audience;
- action;
- time and cash cap;
- success, weak-signal, and stop thresholds;
- what decision follows each result.

## Safety Boundaries

- Do not guarantee earnings or employability.
- Do not recommend quitting, borrowing, paid advertising, inventory, or many subscriptions before evidence supports it.
- Do not provide professional legal, medical, tax, or investment conclusions.
- Do not expose employer, client, customer, or personal secrets.
- Do not invent market size, pricing, competitors, or platform rules. Research them only with permission and current sources.
