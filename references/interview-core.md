# Shared Interview Framework

Use the same seven modules for all three tracks. Track files adapt the questions; they do not change the diagnostic model.

## How to Ask Deeply

For each useful claim, move from a label to a scene:

1. **Reflect:** summarize the user's meaning in one or two sentences.
2. **Notice:** name one possible signal and one uncertainty.
3. **Ask:** pose one specific main question.
4. **Support recall:** suggest a few kinds of examples without answering for the user.

Example:

> You described repeat clients, renewals, and people returning for advice. These may share a trust-building pattern, but we do not yet know what you actually do differently. Think of one person who was initially unsure and later trusted you: what changed, what did you do, and what did they do afterward?

Avoid abstract prompts such as “What are your strengths?” until concrete evidence has been gathered.

## Seven Shared Modules

### 1. Experience and roles — 1–2 questions

Discover significant work, life, volunteer, caregiving, hobby, community, or entrepreneurial experience. Clarify duration, responsibility, context, and transitions.

### 2. Tasks and problem solving — 2 questions

Identify what the user personally did, which problems they owned, how decisions were made, and what changed because of their actions. Separate personal contribution from team or market effects.

### 3. Outcomes and proof — 2 questions

Look for numbers, before-and-after differences, completed work, retained customers, reduced cost, time saved, adoption, repeatability, or durable artifacts. Approximate ranges are acceptable when clearly labeled as estimates.

### 4. External recognition and requests — 1–2 questions

Ask who seeks the user's help, what they ask for, whether advice is adopted, and whether anyone has referred, returned, promoted, paid, or offered access. Distinguish compliments from behavioral signals.

### 5. Relative ease and learning speed — 1–2 questions

Find work the user sustains, learns, notices, or completes more naturally than peers. Test whether “easy” comes from accumulated expertise, access, temperament, a repeatable method, or simply greater effort.

### 6. Transferable assets — 1–2 questions

Inventory knowledge, relationships, audience, reputation, distribution, equipment, location, data, reusable materials, licenses, tools, and weekly time. An asset need not produce income yet.

### 7. Goals, constraints, and risk — 2 questions

Clarify the 90-day outcome, available hours, cash budget, minimum income need, health and family constraints, risk tolerance, disliked activities, ethical boundaries, and what the user will not do.

## Deepening Prompts

Use only when they address a real gap:

- What happened immediately before and after?
- What did you personally decide or produce?
- What did other people do differently because of it?
- How do you know your action contributed to the result?
- Has a similar pattern happened elsewhere?
- When did this approach fail or become exhausting?
- What part could another person copy, and what depends on you?
- What evidence would change your current belief?

## Conversation Quality Checks

- The user should feel understood before being challenged.
- Reflections should be brief; do not turn every answer into a mini-report.
- Challenge causal claims gently and specifically.
- Do not diagnose personality, mental health, intelligence, or innate talent.
- If legal, medical, financial, or confidential issues appear, extract only transferable skills and state the professional or privacy boundary.
